# Jobs-to-be-Done Analysis — Top-Ranked Segments
## Phase 3.2 companion to the Situation-Based Segmentation. Prepared 13 August 2026.

**Scope.** The seven highest-composite segments from the segmentation (composite ≥ 1,920; the ranking's natural break falls between rank 7 at 1,920 and rank 8 at 1,728): (1) Nitaqat-squeezed Saudi employer, (2) scale-up outgrown structure, (3) AI-investment decision paralysis, (4) individual Arabic HR/OD professionals, (5) ERP rollout collision, (6) Vision-program execution gap, (7) post-restructuring implementation failure.

**Method discipline.** The customer's stated problem is treated as unproven until evidence supports it. Emotional and social/political jobs are almost never stated by buyers; every one below that is not derived from direct evidence is labeled **INFERENCE**. Where more than one "real job" is plausible, competing hypotheses are stated and left unresolved, per stop conditions. No segment is recommended.

**Evidence base.** Phase 2.1 Market Scan; segmentation table (Phase 3.1); Unfair Advantage Map; founder Block A–G responses. Note the structural evidence limit: **no buyer in any segment has been interviewed.** Every emotional/political job in this document is therefore inference or hypothesis by construction, and this analysis is a set of interview instruments as much as a set of findings.

## Segment 1 — Nitaqat-squeezed Saudi private employer (buyer: CHRO)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Restructure roles and org design so the company reaches/keeps Green–Platinum Nitaqat status across the 269 quota professions without breaking operations. |
| Emotional job | Relief from regulatory fear; sleeping at night knowing the downgrade risk is handled. **INFERENCE.** |
| Strategic job | Convert a compliance burden into a defensible workforce structure that survives the 2026–2028 tightening cycle — solve it once, not every quota wave. |
| Social/political job | The CHRO demonstrates command to CEO/board — compliance as proof of competence; avoids being the named cause of visa blocks. **INFERENCE.** |
| Trigger | Mutawar effective date (26 Apr 2026), Qiwa notice, band-downgrade warning, a peer firm's penalty becoming known. |
| Desired outcome | Safe band status + an org structure that still functions; a Saudization narrative usable with regulators. |
| Current workaround | Quota gaming (ghost/phantom Saudization), ad-hoc re-titling, expat hiring freezes, compliance paperwork consultants. |
| Why workaround is painful | Gaming carries penalty and audit risk under Qiwa digital documentation; re-titling corrupts the org design; freezes starve operations; paperwork firms don't touch structure. |
| What causes search | A dated threshold they cannot game past; escalation from government-relations; CFO quantifying the cost of a downgrade. |
| What blocks buying | Belief HR can absorb it; fear an outsider will see the gaming already done; cost vs. "just hire more Saudis." |
| What creates urgency | Statutory dates — the only segment with a calendar the buyer doesn't control. |
| What creates budget | Non-discretionary compliance spend; CFO releases funds when penalty exposure is quantified. |
| What creates trust | KSA-specific delivery record, fluency in Nitaqat/Qiwa mechanics, Arabic delivery, references from similar-size Saudi firms. |
| What creates risk | Advisor learning non-compliant practices; recommendations that force out expats who actually run operations; disruption during redesign. |

## Segment 2 — Scale-up that outgrew its structure (buyer: CEO/COO)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Design structure, roles, and decision rights that let the company operate without routing everything through founders. |
| Emotional job | Founder relief from being the bottleneck; discharge of guilt over chaos-driven attrition. **INFERENCE.** |
| Strategic job | Institutionalize the company beyond its founders; be structurally credible for the next round or expansion. |
| Social/political job | Redistribute decision rights away from specific people (including the founder) with a neutral third party absorbing the resentment; signal maturity to investors. **INFERENCE.** |
| Trigger | Funding round, senior-exec resignation, investor/board comment on "organizational maturity," a visible operational breakdown. |
| Desired outcome | A structure people actually follow; founder time freed; investors reassured; senior hires who stay. |
| Current workaround | Hiring a COO/CHRO to "sort it out," copying org models from admired companies, intuition-based reorgs. |
| Why workaround is painful | Imported models misfit; intuition reorgs churn and don't stick; new executives inherit ambiguity and quit — the problem consumes its own solutions. |
| What causes search | Attrition of people they can't afford to lose; direct investor pressure; a breakdown with a customer or regulator. |
| What blocks buying | Founder conviction that nobody knows the company like they do; cost compared against "just hire a strong COO"; fear of losing control — the exact failure mode in the founder's implementation diagnosis. |
| What creates urgency | Funding milestones and exec exodus; otherwise chronically postponable. |
| What creates budget | Round proceeds; a board mandate converting "org debt" into a funded workstream. |
| What creates trust | Operator credibility — having lived inside a scaling company (the Geidea analogue), not just advised one; speed; founder-to-founder register. |
| What creates risk | Consultant installs big-company bureaucracy that kills speed; the diagnosis lands on the founder's own role. **INFERENCE.** |

## Segment 3 — Executive team in AI-investment decision paralysis (buyer: CSO/Transformation head)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Converge on which AI use cases to fund, in what sequence, against what criteria, with named owners. |
| Emotional job | Relief from the double fear — betting wrong versus being left behind; escape from FOMO-driven vendor noise. **INFERENCE.** |
| Strategic job | A defensible AI capital-allocation position the board accepts; avoid the stalled-pilot graveyard the scan documents. |
| Social/political job | A decision made "by process" distributes accountability — no single executive owns the blame if a bet fails; may also break an IT-vs-business factional deadlock without forcing the CEO to pick a side. **INFERENCE.** |
| Trigger | Board asks "what is our AI strategy?"; annual budget cycle; competitor announcement; audit of pilot spend. |
| Desired outcome | A funded, sequenced AI portfolio signed off at board level — and pilots actually killed, not just new ones added. |
| Current workaround | Vendor POCs, internal AI committees and offsites, hiring a Chief AI Officer, buying a Big 4 roadmap. |
| Why workaround is painful | Committees align without deciding; vendor pilots optimize the vendor's product; roadmaps shelf-ware (the scan: deliverable value eroding); a CAIO hire takes quarters and doesn't resolve the political deadlock. |
| What causes search | A budget deadline that forces choices; a written-off pilot; leadership change resetting the mandate. |
| What blocks buying | The buyer frames the gap as *knowledge* ("we need AI expertise") not *process* — and buys substance advisors instead; "we need a strategy, not a workshop" skepticism; offsite fatigue. |
| What creates urgency | Budget cycles and the Year-of-AI mandate environment; urgency attaches to the topic, not yet to the decision process. |
| What creates budget | AI allocations exist (⅔ of GCC firms expect >30% of consulting spend on AI); facilitation must attach itself to that budget line — the budget-holder for "decision process" as such is unmapped. |
| What creates trust | Evidence the facilitator understands AI substance, not process alone; peer references; Arabic delivery for the room where the real decision-makers sit. |
| What creates risk | A workshop that produces a poster, not a decision; exposing executive disagreement in front of peers; the implicit admission that the leadership team needed help deciding. **INFERENCE.** |

## Segment 4 — Individual Arabic-speaking HR/OD professionals (buyer: the professional)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Acquire structured OD knowledge and tools in Arabic, applicable at work. |
| Emotional job | Professional confidence; relief from AI-era skill anxiety; pride in mastering a discipline in one's own language. **INFERENCE.** |
| Strategic job | Career progression — promotion, a move into OD/consulting, employability as Saudization expands HR roles. |
| Social/political job | Being legible as "the OD person" — visible expertise on LinkedIn and in the room; credential signaling to employers. **INFERENCE.** |
| Trigger | Promotion opportunity, new HR mandate, job insecurity, watching peers post certificates. |
| Desired outcome | An applicable toolkit plus a visible, employer-recognized marker of expertise. |
| Current workaround | Free content, YouTube, ChatGPT, English certifications (CIPD/SHRM), generic Arabic management courses. |
| Why workaround is painful | English is a genuine barrier at depth; free content is unstructured; generic Arabic courses lack OD specificity; ChatGPT gives answers, not competence or credentials. |
| What causes search | Career events with money attached — interviews, promotion cases, salary reviews. |
| What blocks buying | Abundant free alternatives; a certificate nobody recognizes is worth little; personal price sensitivity (Egypt especially). |
| What creates urgency | Low and event-driven; self-paced learning is always postponable. |
| What creates budget | Personal funds; occasionally employer L&D reimbursement — which changes the required proof (invoice-ability, recognized certification). |
| What creates trust | The instructor's visible practice record (the 18k-follower content channel is the trust engine); testimonials; Arabic-native fluency in OD terminology. |
| What creates risk | Wasted money and evenings; a credential that carries no weight with employers. |

## Segment 5 — Enterprise system rollout colliding with the operating model (buyer: CIO/Transformation director)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Rescue adoption: redesign roles, workflows, and decision rights so the system's process model and the organization stop fighting. |
| Emotional job | Survival — the program carries the buyer's name and the slippage is visible; fear has a date on it. **INFERENCE.** |
| Strategic job | Realize the benefits case that justified the investment; avoid a write-off. |
| Social/political job | Re-attribute the failure narrative from "IT delivered badly" to "the organization wasn't redesigned for it" — which changes who owns the fix. **INFERENCE.** |
| Trigger | Missed go-live, steering-committee escalation, post-go-live user revolt, auditor comments. |
| Desired outcome | Adoption, benefits realization, and the program publicly declared a success. |
| Current workaround | More training, integrator OCM add-ons, super-user programs, blaming users. |
| Why workaround is painful | Training cannot fix structural misfit; the integrator's incentive is configuration and license scope, not org change; blame corrodes the program coalition. |
| What causes search | Escalation above the buyer's head — CEO/board attention makes doing nothing more dangerous than bringing in help. |
| What blocks buying | Program budget already exhausted; adding an advisor mid-crisis admits the original scope was wrong; the integrator insists OCM is covered. |
| What creates urgency | Go-live dates and quantified cost-of-delay. |
| What creates budget | Rescue/contingency budgets that appear only after slippage is visible — budget exists precisely when pain peaks. |
| What creates trust | Having *owned* a rollout from inside (the Geidea SAP story is the exact credential), not consulted on one; speaking both the IT and the org language. |
| What creates risk | Another consultant on a failing program is another blame surface; org redesign mid-program adds disruption to a system already on fire. |

## Segment 6 — Vision-program entity with a strategy-execution gap (buyer: Strategy/PMO director)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Build the cascade — scorecard, KPI system, decision cadence — that converts the strategy into execution against program KPIs. |
| Emotional job | Relief from accountability exposure in program reviews; not being the red square on the ministry dashboard. **INFERENCE.** |
| Strategic job | Secure the entity's standing and next funding cycle with its supervisors. |
| Social/political job | Produce *demonstrable* execution machinery — the artifact shown upward matters at least as much as the machinery working. **INFERENCE.** |
| Trigger | Red KPIs in a review, supervisory criticism, new entity leadership wanting a reset. |
| Desired outcome | Green (or credibly greening) KPIs plus a visible governance apparatus that satisfies reviewers. |
| Current workaround | PMO bodyshop staffing, reporting tools, internal task forces. |
| Why workaround is painful | Bodyshops report status but cannot force decisions; reporting becomes theatre; task forces dissolve into line duties. |
| What causes search | The review calendar — externally imposed and non-negotiable. |
| What blocks buying | Incumbent firms already embedded; procurement friction (Etimad, panels); the internal PMO's defensiveness — an external fixing execution implies the PMO failed. |
| What creates urgency | Review cycles and program-funding gates. |
| What creates budget | Program budgets with execution lines that survive ROI scrutiny better than new strategy work (scan: spend shifting formulation→execution). |
| What creates trust | A scorecard/cascade delivery record, government-context fluency, Arabic, and discretion. |
| What creates risk | An outsider exposing the true state of execution to supervisors; blame flowing to whoever hired them. **INFERENCE.** |

## Segment 7 — Post-restructuring implementation failure (buyer: CEO, burned)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Functional job | Make the restructure they already paid for actually happen. |
| Emotional job | Resentment toward consultants; embarrassment that an approved design went nowhere; creeping fear the organization cannot change at all. **INFERENCE.** |
| Strategic job | Capture value already bought; restore internal credibility of change itself (the founder's diagnosis: failed restructuring breeds disbelief in restructuring). |
| Social/political job | Two conflicting political jobs may coexist: (a) an external forcing function for decisions the CEO has been avoiding — with the facilitator absorbing the discomfort; (b) vindication — an account of the failure that does not implicate the CEO's own avoidance. **INFERENCE, both.** |
| Trigger | A second flare-up (attrition, cost, dysfunction), a board member asking about the shelved report, a new CFO asking what the fees bought. |
| Desired outcome | Implemented structure without re-litigating the past; face preserved. |
| Current workaround | Internal task forces, partial implementation, quiet shelving. |
| Why workaround is painful | Internal teams lack authority to force decisions on peers; partial implementation creates hybrid dysfunction worse than either state. |
| What causes search | Often nothing — the pain normalizes. Search requires a fresh crisis colliding with the memory of the sunk fee. |
| What blocks buying | Consulting fatigue is the defining trait: "we tried that." Buying again re-opens the wound and re-exposes the original decision to hire the first firm. |
| What creates urgency | Low between flare-ups; spikes are event-driven. |
| What creates budget | Budget exists (they paid once); the CFO now demands outcome linkage or guarantees. |
| What creates trust | Naming their failure mode accurately *before being told* — the founder's four-part diagnosis is the entry key; outcome-linked pricing; refusal to sell a new diagnosis when one exists. |
| What creates risk | Paying twice for the same failure; the new advisor discovering that the block was the CEO's own avoidance — the buyer may half-know this. **INFERENCE.** |

## OUTLINE — The underlying "real job" per segment, with confirm/kill tests

Stated problems are hypotheses. Where two real-job readings are plausible, both are stated and neither is chosen.

**1. Nitaqat-squeezed employer.**
- H1 — *Risk transfer:* "Keep us legal without breaking the machine" — the hire is compliance insurance, and structural quality is incidental. → Scope collapses once band status is safe; buyer resists paying for anything beyond the quota math.
- H2 — *Forcing function:* the regulation is the CHRO's lever to force structural decisions leadership has resisted for years; the consultant is ammunition. **INFERENCE.**
- Confirm/kill: in buyer interviews, ask what happens after Green status is reached. H2 lives if the CHRO's wish-list extends past compliance; H1 if the engagement is framed purely as band arithmetic. Kill for both: firms simply buy quota-gaming services and accept the risk.

**2. Scale-up outgrown structure.**
- H1 — *Safe redistribution of power:* "Take decision rights away from me/others without me becoming the villain" — a neutral authority absorbs the politics. **INFERENCE.**
- H2 — *Legitimacy artifact:* "Prove to investors we are a real company" — the deliverable's audience is the board, and implementation is secondary.
- Confirm/kill: track whether the structure is *enacted* (rights actually move) or *presented* (deck to board, then drift). The founder's own implementation-failure diagnosis predicts H1 engagements stall exactly at the control-transfer moment — making this segment a live test of her central thesis.

**3. AI-investment decision paralysis.**
- H1 — *Accountability distribution:* a decision made "by process" means no individual owns the blame for a failed AI bet. **INFERENCE.**
- H2 — *Deadlock breaking:* the real client is a CEO who will not adjudicate between factions; the workshop is arbitration in disguise. **INFERENCE.**
- H3 — *The stated job is real:* the team genuinely lacks a decision method for a technology none of them can evaluate.
- Confirm/kill: this is precisely what the Phase 2.1 Recommendation-1 interviews must discriminate — whether buyers describe a knowledge gap (they'll buy substance experts; kills the facilitation thesis), an alignment gap (H2/H3 live), or a defensibility gap (H1 lives). Kill signal already visible in market: buyers defaulting to Big 4 substance advisory instead of process help.

**4. Individual HR/OD professionals.**
- H1 — *Identity/credential job:* "Make me legible as an OD professional" — recognition is the product; content is the vehicle. **INFERENCE.**
- H2 — *Knowledge job:* Arabic-first learners genuinely cannot get OD depth elsewhere; content is the product.
- Confirm/kill: cheapest test in the entire portfolio — one promotion to the existing list with two framings (certificate-led vs. skills-led) and compare conversion. The 100% completion rate (small n) leans H2, but completers were likely pre-committed followers.

**5. ERP rollout collision.**
- H1 — *Rescue + re-attribution:* save the program *and* the buyer's position by reframing failure as organizational rather than technical — the fix and the narrative are one purchase. **INFERENCE.**
- H2 — *Pure functional rescue:* adoption is genuinely the goal; politics incidental.
- Confirm/kill: whether the buyer wants the diagnosis socialized upward (H1 — the reframe is the point) or fixed quietly (H2). If H1 dominates, the seller's real deliverable includes a defensible account of the past, which changes the offer materially.

**6. Vision-program execution gap.**
- H1 — *Audit defense:* demonstrable machinery that protects the entity in reviews — the upward-facing artifact is the job. **INFERENCE.**
- H2 — *Genuine execution build:* cascade, decision cadence, and rhythm are actually wanted.
- Confirm/kill: observe what the buyer inspects in delivery — if review-pack quality gets more attention than decision-cadence adoption, H1. Both can be true sequentially (H1 buys entry; H2 sustains the relationship) — that sequence, if real, matters more than either hypothesis alone.

**7. Post-restructuring failure.**
- H1 — *External forcing function:* "Force the decisions I've been avoiding, and let me attribute the discomfort to the process." **INFERENCE.**
- H2 — *Vindication:* "Show the failure wasn't my leadership." **INFERENCE.**
- These conflict irreconcilably: H1 requires confronting the CEO's avoidance; H2 requires absolving it. A seller who guesses wrong loses the engagement either way. Confirm/kill: early reference conversations — does the buyer's account of the failed engagement include their own role? If yes, H1 is workable. If the account is purely consultant-blaming, H2 is operative and the engagement is likely unwinnable on honest terms.

**Cross-segment observation — flagged for bias before it is used.** Five of seven real-job hypotheses (2, 3, 5, 6, 7) reduce to variants of one job: *make a politically dangerous decision safe — by distributing accountability, absorbing resentment, or re-attributing failure.* This convergence is consistent with the founder's implementation-failure diagnosis and with the facilitated-decision-design thesis. It is also exactly the pattern this engagement is predisposed to find, since the analyst applying the JTBD lens knows the thesis. **INFERENCE at risk of confirmation bias** — treat the convergence as a hypothesis the buyer interviews must be *designed to falsify* (ask buyers what they'd pay for without ever mentioning decisions or facilitation), not as a finding. Per stop conditions, no segment is recommended and no ambiguity above has been resolved by convenience.
